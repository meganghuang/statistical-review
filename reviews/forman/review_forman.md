# Statistical review — Forman, Renner & Leavens (2023), *Fetching felines*

**Paper.** Forman J, Renner E, Leavens DA (2023). Fetching felines: a survey of cat owners on the diversity of cat (*Felis catus*) fetching behaviour. *Scientific Reports* **13**:20456. doi:10.1038/s41598-023-47409-w

**Data reviewed.** The publisher's Supplementary Information, as deposited: the per-cat dataset `41598_2023_47409_MOESM3_ESM.csv` (1154 rows × 75 columns), the authors' annotated R script (MOESM2), the questionnaire (MOESM1), the thematic-map and quotation supplements (MOESM4, MOESM5, MOESM7) and the object spreadsheet (MOESM6). Every number below was recomputed from MOESM3 in this session.

**Scope.** 35 claims: every sentence in the Results, Abstract and Discussion that asserts a difference, an association or the absence of one, including the four null results and the three interpretive claims the Abstract and Summary rest on. Purely descriptive counts that assert nothing (median age at first fetch, country breakdown, favourite-object frequencies) are not in the register.

## 2. Summary

**This is a competently analysed paper whose reported numbers reproduce.** Every chi-square, Kruskal–Wallis, pairwise Wilcoxon and Mann–Whitney statistic in the Results was recomputed from the deposited per-cat file and matched the published value to the printed precision, with one transcription-level exception (§3, F-9). Group counts match exactly: 553 / 352 / 249 for who initiates, 675 / 231 / 248 for who ends, 617 / 537 for sex, 1089 / 65 for training status. The authors' R script is complete enough to re-run, their outlier-sensitivity analyses are in it, and their post-hoc comparisons were Bonferroni-corrected within family. 26 of 35 claims are **Supported**; 19 of those carry no defect at all, and the other 7 (C4c, C5a, C7a, C9–C12) carry only a severity-C reporting defect — an absence claim stated without an interval (F-10) — which does not disturb the conclusion. The central result — that owners report their cats, not themselves, as the usual agent that starts and stops a fetching session — is robust by a wide margin: it would survive an owner-clustering correction up to roughly 20-fold (initiation) and 34-fold (termination), where the design can require at most about 1.8-fold.

**Three things change how the paper should be read.** First, every test treats the 1154 cats as independent when they were reported by 924 owners, so between 1.4× and 1.8× of variance inflation is unaccounted for depending on how strongly cats within an owner resemble each other. That penalty is far too small to touch the headline claims, but it is exactly large enough to remove the one marginal result in the paper — "significantly more male fetching cats than female" (p = 0.0185 naive; p = 0.046 to 0.079 once clustering is allowed for) — which also fails Bonferroni correction across the paper's eleven primary tests (p = 0.204). That sentence should not be read as a finding. Second, the paper reports no effect size and no interval for any of its twenty-one significant results; the authors' own code contains no effect-size call, and the Mann–Whitney tests were run with `conf.int = TRUE` so a Hodges–Lehmann interval was computed and then not reported. Recomputed here, every rank comparison in the paper sits between 0.28 and 0.65 on the probability-of-superiority scale, i.e. the strongest association in the paper corresponds to a 72:28 bet, not a categorical separation. Third, one reported pairwise difference compares two groups with **identical medians** (5 retrievals vs 5 retrievals) and is written as a median comparison; a rank-sum test does not license that sentence.

**What is not wrong here.** The sampling frame is self-selected and recruited through social media, but the authors say so, scope their conclusions to owners of fetching cats, and explicitly disclaim prevalence inference in the Discussion. The choice of nonparametric tests on heavily right-skewed counts is defensible on its own terms even though the selection procedure was mechanical. Unequal group sizes, the absence of a multiplicity correction *across* families of genuinely distinct pre-stated questions, descriptive reporting without tests, and the retention of extreme outliers after a documented sensitivity check are all acceptable. There is no p-hacking signature: of the 21 significant results, 19 have p ≤ 0.001 and only one falls in (0.01, 0.05]; the p-value distribution is strongly right-skewed with no pile-up below alpha.

## 3. Findings

### F-1 — Severity A — The male-biased sex ratio (C2) does not survive its own design

**What was done.** The 617 male and 537 female cats were compared with a chi-square goodness-of-fit test against a 50:50 expectation, df = 1, reported as X² = 5.50, p = 0.018, and stated in the Results as "There were significantly more male fetching cats than female fetching cats".

**What it assumes.** That the 1154 cats are 1154 independent draws, and that 0.5 is the right reference proportion for the population the claim is about.

**Why it fails here.** Both assumptions break. (i) The 1154 cats were reported by 924 owners. Cats sharing an owner share a rater, and in many cases a household; the deposited file flags 286 of the 1154 rows as entries where the owner's demographics were repeated from a previous cat. Under the first-order Rao–Scott correction the statistic must be divided by the design effect. Given 924 owners and 1154 cats, the design effect at the worst case of perfect within-owner correlation lies between 1.399 (the least concentrated arrangement consistent with those totals: 230 owners with two cats each) and 1.794 (the most concentrated arrangement consistent with the largest household reported in the data, which is four fetching cats). The claim stops clearing α = 0.05 at a design effect of **1.444** — inside that range. Adjusted, p = 0.046 at the low end and p = 0.079 at the high end. (ii) The sample was recruited by asking owners of cats that already fetch to volunteer on Twitter, Facebook and Reddit. The sex composition of such a sample is determined jointly by the sex ratio of owned cats in the responding countries, by which owners saw the post, and by which cats they chose to describe. A test against 0.5 therefore has no population behind it — it is a statement about the composition of the respondent pool, and no re-analysis can repair that. (iii) It is additionally the only one of the paper's eleven primary tests that fails Bonferroni correction across that family (adjusted p = 0.204), while surviving Benjamini–Hochberg (q = 0.029).

**What should be done.** Report the composition descriptively — 53.5% male, 95% CI 50.6–56.3% — and drop the test, or test against an external reference ratio for owned cats in the sampled countries with owner-clustered standard errors.

**What changes.** The sentence "There were significantly more male fetching cats than female fetching cats" should be removed from the Results, and the corresponding Discussion sentence reduced to a description of the sample. Nothing else in the paper depends on it; the four sex-comparison nulls (C9–C12) are unaffected.

### F-2 — Severity B — A reported "significant difference" between two groups with the same median (C5b)

**What was done.** "There are significantly fewer retrievals if the owner usually initiates the sessions (median = 5, IQR = 4) compared to the cat (median = 5, IQR = 6, Pairwise Wilcoxon p = 0.009)."

**What it assumes.** That a significant Wilcoxon rank-sum test licenses a statement about medians. It does not: the rank-sum test's null is that the two distributions are identical, and translating a significant U into a median shift requires the extra assumption of a pure location shift with a shared shape.

**Why it fails here.** The two medians are printed in the same sentence and they are the same number. The groups differ in the upper tail, not in central location: the probability that a randomly chosen cat-initiated dyad exceeds a randomly chosen owner-initiated one is 0.565, 95% bootstrap CI [0.522, 0.604] — a small ordering, not a shift of the median. This is also the weakest of the paper's twelve pairwise comparisons against clustering: it stops clearing its Bonferroni threshold of α = 0.0167 at a design effect of **1.525**, so under the more concentrated clustering scenario (design effect 1.794) the adjusted p is 0.027 and the comparison is no longer significant; under the least concentrated scenario (1.399) it is 0.012 and survives.

**What should be done.** State the comparison in the quantity the test estimates — "cats initiated sessions with somewhat more retrievals, probability of superiority 0.57 [0.52, 0.60]" — and note that the medians are equal. If a median claim is wanted, estimate the Hodges–Lehmann shift with an interval instead.

**What changes.** The direction of the effect is unchanged and the omnibus Kruskal–Wallis for this outcome (C5) remains significant under every clustering scenario considered (worst case p = 0.020). What changes is the strength: this is a tail difference of modest size, borderline under clustering, not a difference in the typical number of retrievals.

### F-3 — Severity B — The Abstract sentence claims a quantity that was not measured (C13)

**What was done.** The Abstract states "Cats initiated and terminated fetching bouts more often than did their owners", supported by the conditional chi-square tests C3a and C4a.

**What it assumes.** That a per-dyad categorical judgement of the usual agent is interchangeable with a rate over bouts.

**Why it fails here.** The survey asked who initiates and who ends sessions *on average*, with three options (cat / about equal / owner). No bout was counted. The tests behind the claim additionally condition on discarding the "about equal" responses — 30.5% of dyads for initiation and 20.0% for termination — so "more often than did their owners" is a statement about the 802 and 923 non-tied dyads, not about all 1154. Unconditionally the split is 47.9% cat vs 21.6% owner (initiation) and 58.5% vs 21.5% (termination); conditionally it is 69.0% [65.7–72.1] and 73.1% [70.2–75.9]. The direction is the same under either reading, and both tests are enormously robust to clustering (breakdown design effects of 20.1 and 34.5).

**What should be done.** Write the sentence in the measured quantity: "Owners reported their cat, rather than themselves, as the usual initiator of fetching sessions in 47.9% of dyads (vs 21.6% for the owner) and as the usual terminator in 58.5% (vs 21.5%)."

**What changes.** Nothing in the statistical support; the strength and the referent of the claim become accurate. The abstract currently reads as a behavioural rate measurement when it is an owner-perception survey.

### F-4 — Severity B — "Agency" and "control" are interpretive, not measured (C14)

**What was done.** The Abstract concludes that "cats who fetch demonstrate independent and co-ordinated agency in the onset and maintenance of fetching behaviour", and the Summary that "the agency of fetching lies predominantly with the cat, who is largely in control of a fetching session".

**What it assumes.** That owner-attributed initiation maps onto cat agency, and that the association between the attributed initiator and the number of retrievals reflects a causal influence of the cat on the session.

**Why it fails here.** The design is cross-sectional, unblinded owner report with no randomisation and no behavioural observation; who initiates was not manipulated. The association in C5–C8 is equally consistent with the reverse direction (owners who find their cat fetches a lot come to describe the cat as the initiator) and with a third factor (an enthusiastic cat produces both more retrievals and a stronger impression of cat-led play). The Discussion itself offers two post-hoc causal readings in opposite directions — owners forced to end long sessions, and owners prolonging sessions beyond the cat's inclination — which is a sign that the design does not distinguish them. No quantity in this dataset estimates agency.

**What should be done.** Scope the conclusion to perception: "owners perceive their cats as the usual agent initiating and terminating fetching sessions, and that perception covaries with reported session intensity". Keep the agency framing in the Introduction and Future Directions, where it is a motivation rather than a result.

**What changes.** The inferential claims C3–C8 are untouched. The Abstract's final sentence becomes a hypothesis the paper motivates rather than a finding it establishes.

### F-5 — Severity B — "No differences" asserted from p = 1.00 (C8c)

**What was done.** "There were no differences in the number of fetching sessions per month between the owner ending the sessions or whether this was about equal (Pairwise Wilcoxon p = 1.00)."

**What it assumes.** That a large p-value is evidence of equivalence. A Bonferroni-adjusted p of 1.00 is a failure to detect, not a demonstration of no difference, and equivalence requires a pre-stated margin.

**Why it fails here.** The two groups are not small (n = 248 and 231) and the point estimate is close to null, but the interval is not negligible: probability of superiority 0.510, 95% bootstrap CI [0.458, 0.561], with medians of 10 and 12 sessions per month. The data are compatible with a real ordering as strong as 0.56 in either direction — larger than several differences the paper elsewhere calls significant.

**What should be done.** Write "no evidence of a difference" and show the interval, or pre-state an equivalence margin and test against it.

**What changes.** The result remains null. The sentence stops asserting that the two groups are the same.

### F-6 — Severity B — Not assessable: the Siamese prevalence claim has no denominator (C16)

**What was done.** The Discussion reports 36 Siamese among 160 purebreds as "consistent with what previous research has found in relation to the Siamese and crossbreed variations being amongst the most common fetching breeds", and proposes future work "to help determine whether fetching is more common amongst the Siamese purebred or crossbreed variations, as has been supported in our findings".

**What it assumes.** That the breed composition of a sample recruited exclusively from owners of fetching cats carries information about which breeds fetch more often.

**Why it fails here.** It cannot. A claim of the form "breed X fetches more" requires P(fetches | breed X) and therefore the breed distribution of *non-fetching* owned cats, which this survey deliberately excludes. The observed 36 Siamese is a numerator with no denominator; the same count would arise from Siamese being unusually prevalent among respondents, from Siamese owners being more likely to know their cat's breed (86.1% of the sample is "mixed breed", largely because owners did not state a breed), or from Siamese genuinely fetching more. The paper partly acknowledges this — it calls the purebred sample small and non-representative — but then treats the ranking as supporting the prior literature.

**What should be done.** Report the count as a description of the sample and remove "as has been supported in our findings". A prevalence claim needs a design that samples cats, not fetching cats.

**What changes.** The sentence becomes descriptive. The paper's stated limitation about prevalence already covers it; the Discussion sentence currently contradicts that limitation.

### F-7 — Severity C — No effect size or interval is reported for any of the 21 significant results

**What was done.** Every inferential result in the paper is reported as a test statistic and a p-value. Chi-squares are given without Cramér's V or a proportion interval; Kruskal–Wallis without ε² or η²; pairwise Wilcoxon and Mann–Whitney without a rank-biserial correlation, probability of superiority or Hodges–Lehmann shift. A search of the authors' R script finds no effect-size call of any kind; the two Mann–Whitney tests were run with `conf.int = TRUE`, so a Hodges–Lehmann interval was computed in the session and not carried into the paper.

**What it assumes.** That a p-value answers the question. It does not: with n = 1154 a trivial ordering clears α comfortably, and the reader cannot tell a 0.57 probability of superiority from a 0.28 one when both are printed as "p < 0.001".

**Why it fails here.** It materially changes how the Results read. Recomputed, the twelve pairwise comparisons span probability of superiority 0.284 to 0.653 — a factor-of-two range in strength all reported with the same "p < 0.001". The strongest result in the paper (more retrievals when the owner ends the session than when the cat does) is 0.284 [0.248, 0.322], i.e. a 72:28 ordering; the weakest significant one (F-2) is 0.565. The Kruskal–Wallis effect sizes likewise range over an order of magnitude: ε² = 0.010 for retrievals by initiator against 0.094 for retrievals by ender.

**What should be done.** Report probability of superiority (U / n₁n₂) with a bootstrap interval for every rank test, ε² for every Kruskal–Wallis, and a proportion with a Wilson interval for every chi-square.

**What changes.** No verdict changes. The reader gains the ability to rank the paper's findings by strength, which the current reporting prevents — and the weakest of them turns out to be the one in the sentence quoted above (F-2).

### F-8 — Severity C — Tests selected by preliminary tests of their own assumptions

**What was done.** "Normality checks were carried out using Shapiro–Wilk tests and assessing QQ plots prior to statistical analysis… The Shapiro–Wilk tests determined the distributions of the data were significantly different from a normal distribution, thus non-parametric tests were used." Separately, "Levene's tests were used to determine the most suitable adjustment method in post-hoc analyses which showed there were not equal variances (p < 0.05). Thus, pairwise Wilcoxon rank sum tests with Bonferroni corrections were used".

**What it assumes.** That a preliminary significance test is a valid selector for the primary test. Conditioning the analysis on a data-dependent screen distorts the error rate of whatever follows, and at these group sizes (n = 231 to 675 per cell) Shapiro–Wilk will reject departures from normality that a rank test and a mean-based test would both tolerate.

**Why it fails here.** It is a forking path rather than a wrong answer. The outcomes are counts bounded below at zero with pronounced right skew (retrievals: median 5, maximum 68; monthly sessions: median 8, maximum 180), so a nonparametric analysis is the right choice on the shape of the data and the sample size — it simply was not chosen on those grounds. The Levene-based selection is more awkward, because unequal variances across the initiator and ender groups are themselves part of the finding (the interquartile ranges differ by a factor of two) and a rank test does not address a spread difference.

**What should be done.** Justify the test from the measurement type, the skew visible in Figure 1 and the sample size, not from a screening p-value; and, since the comparison is between skewed counts, consider a count model (negative binomial with owner random effects) that would deliver both a clustered standard error and an interpretable rate ratio.

**What changes.** No result changes — the nonparametric analyses chosen are appropriate for these data. The justification needs rewriting, and the forking path should be disclosed.

### F-9 — Severity C — Reporting slips and a missing owner identifier in the deposited data

**What was done.** Three items. (i) The sex-ratio statistic is printed as X² = 5.50; recomputation gives X² = 5.546 (the reported p of 0.018 matches the recomputed 0.0185, so the discrepancy is in the statistic, not the test). (ii) The Abstract reads "The overwhelming majority (94.4%) of these owners report that fetching emerged in the absence of explicit training", but 94.4% is 1089/1154 *cats*, not a proportion of the 924 owners. (iii) The deposited per-cat file carries no owner or response identifier. The variable `ppl_demographics_repeated` flags 286 rows as repeat entries, but the file's row order does not keep an owner's cats adjacent — a flagged row matches the preceding row's country 46.2% of the time against 42.9% for unflagged rows, i.e. at chance — so the 924 owner clusters cannot be reconstructed.

**What it assumes.** That the reader can verify the arithmetic and re-run the analysis as designed.

**Why it fails here.** (i) and (ii) are transcription-level and change nothing. (iii) is consequential: it is the reason this review can only *bound* the clustering correction (F-1) rather than compute it. The authors' R script is otherwise exemplary — complete, commented, with the outlier-excluded sensitivity analyses included — which makes the omission of a single owner-ID column the one blocking gap in an otherwise reproducible deposit.

**What should be done.** Correct the statistic and the denominator; add an anonymised owner identifier to the deposited file.

**What changes.** F-1 would move from a bounded sensitivity analysis to an exact owner-clustered re-analysis, and the four null results (C9–C12) could be given clustered intervals.

### F-10 — Severity C — Absence claims without intervals (C4c, C5a, C7a, C9–C12, C15, C17)

**What was done.** Eight null results are reported as "there was not a significant difference", "there is no difference in retrieval number between when the cat usually initiates and when initiation is about equal" (C5a, p = 0.995), "no significant difference between the cat and if initiation is about equal" (C7a, p = 0.66), "there were no significant differences between male and female cats", and in the Summary "Fetching is not restricted by the sex, breed or location of the cat". The Discussion adds that sex differences were absent "likely due to fetching being a dyadic play behaviour that does not typically involve multiple cats".

**What it assumes.** That reporting the p-value of a null result is sufficient, and (for the Summary sentence) that factors never tested can be included in a negative claim.

**Why it fails here.** Breed and location were never tested; the Summary sentence lists them alongside sex, which was. And none of the nulls carries an interval, so the reader cannot tell a tightly bounded null from an underpowered one. Recomputed here they are in fact tight: retrievals by sex, probability of superiority 0.472 [0.439, 0.505]; monthly sessions by sex, 0.492 [0.458, 0.526]; owner vs about-equal termination, 51.8% [47.3%, 56.2%]; cat vs about-equal initiation, retrievals 0.481 [0.442, 0.518] and monthly sessions 0.524 [0.484, 0.561]. These are informative nulls and deserve to be reported as such. The post-hoc dyadic explanation for the absence is hedged ("likely") and is not tested by any contrast in these data.

**What should be done.** Report each null with its interval, write "no evidence of a difference" rather than "no difference", and restrict the Summary sentence to the factor that was tested — or state plainly that fetching cats of both sexes, 30 breed groups and 42 countries are represented, which is descriptively true and is what the sentence appears to mean.

**What changes.** No verdict changes. The nulls become usable evidence rather than non-results.

## 5. Limitations of this review

1. **The clustering correction is bounded, not computed.** The deposited data carry no owner identifier and the row order does not preserve owner blocks (F-9), so the 924 owner clusters cannot be reconstructed. Everything in F-1 and F-2 rests on an interval for the design effect derived from the published totals (924 owners, 1154 cats) and the largest household in the data (four fetching cats), evaluated at the worst case of perfect within-owner correlation. The true within-owner correlation is unknown and is certainly below 1, so the true correction lies somewhere between 1 and the band's upper edge. The conclusion that C2 and C5b are the only vulnerable results is therefore robust in the sense that no *other* result can be affected; whether those two actually cross depends on a quantity the deposit does not allow me to estimate.
2. **No pre-registration exists**, so the distinction between pre-specified and exploratory analyses cannot be made from the record. The multiplicity count used here (11 primary tests plus 4 families of 3 post-hoc comparisons) is reconstructed from what was reported, not from what was planned. The survey has 23 questions and the deposited file 75 columns; analyses that were run and not reported cannot be detected. The p-value distribution carries no p-hacking signature (19 of 21 significant results at p ≤ 0.001, one in (0.01, 0.05]), but that is evidence against a pattern, not proof of its absence.
3. **The owner-report measurement cannot be validated.** Nothing in the deposit allows a check of whether owner-attributed initiation corresponds to observed initiation, or whether "number of retrievals in the most recent session" is accurately recalled. F-4 rests on the design, not on a measurement comparison I could perform.
4. **Exclusions cannot be audited.** 334 incomplete responses, 2 cats with "extreme or exaggerated answers", 23 with ambiguous or contradicting responses, and 5 duplicates were removed before deposit; only the surviving 1154 rows are available. The "extreme or exaggerated" rule has no stated numeric threshold, and whether it was applied before or after seeing the analyses cannot be determined. The authors' separate extreme-outlier sensitivity analyses (Q3 + 3×IQR) are in the code and did not change the interpretation, which addresses a different and more tractable question.
5. **Not assessed:** the thematic analysis (MOESM4, MOESM5, MOESM7), the object categorisation in MOESM6, and the geographic figure. These make no inferential claims and were not reviewed. Distributional diagnostics beyond the skew visible in the deposited counts were not computed, because the paper's tests do not rely on normality.
